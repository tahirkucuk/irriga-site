Irrigation Automation and Monitoring

Automation is not about opening a valve remotely. It is about engineering what decides when to open it.

Who Is This Service For

Automation is not right for every field — but for some, it is unavoidable

  • → Vineyards and orchards with many zones where manual management is becoming impractical
  • → Commercial growers who want to take irrigation scheduling out of the realm of guesswork
  • → Greenhouse and soilless farming operations
  • → Businesses that want to manage scattered plots from a single control centre
  • → Operations with restricted water allocation that need to account for every drop
System Architecture

The three layers of automation

In the market, "automation" is sold as a single thing. Yet there are three distinct layers, and most projects buy only the first one and believe they have automation.

1

Layer 1 — Command

Remote or scheduled opening and closing of valves. Field control unit, wireless communication, solar power supply, latching solenoid valves. This layer saves labour. It does not save water — because a human still decides when to irrigate; they just no longer walk to the valve.

2

Layer 2 — Measurement

Continuous measurement and logging of soil moisture, climate, flow, and pressure. This layer provides visibility. On its own it still saves nothing — data accumulates but never becomes a decision. This is the most common situation we encounter in the field: sensors installed, app open, nobody watching.

TRUE AUTOMATION
3

Layer 3 — Decision

The layer where measurement translates into an irrigation decision. This is true automation, and setting it up is an engineering task, not an equipment task: which measurement, at which threshold, triggers which action? Investment made without configuring this layer is nothing more than an expensive remote control.

Decision Approaches

What drives the irrigation decision?

There are four approaches; the right system is usually a combination of them. What we recommend is a hybrid setup: climate data determines how much water to apply, soil moisture measurement confirms and corrects that decision, and flow and pressure measurement verifies that water actually reached its destination.

1. Time-Based

"Every three days for two hours." Simple, inexpensive, predictable. However, it ignores weather conditions and the crop's stage-specific needs.

2. Climate-Based (ET-Based)

Reference crop water consumption is calculated from weather station data and multiplied by the crop coefficient to obtain daily water use. Scientifically sound. Its weakness: it does not see what is actually happening in the soil.

3. Soil Moisture-Based

Moisture in the root zone is measured directly; irrigation is triggered when a defined lower threshold is reached. Measures reality — provided the right point is being measured.

RECOMMENDED
4. Hybrid

A setup where the three approaches above cross-check each other. Climate data determines how much water to apply; soil moisture confirms that decision; flow and pressure verify that water actually got there.

Sensor Placement

The most commonly made mistake

A soil moisture sensor's brand matters far less than where it is placed. The depth, location, and purpose of the measurement determine whether the system makes correct decisions.

Our knowledge here comes not from catalogues but directly from a research background: our founding team participated in the international FLOW-AID and AQUATAG projects targeting low-cost soil moisture sensor development, and conducted doctoral research on root-zone moisture sensing for irrigation management.

Depth

Profile-type probes read the root zone at multiple depths; the representative depth is determined in advance.

Horizontal Position

Distance from the emitter is calculated to remain within the wetted volume; an incorrect distance produces zero readings.

Representative Point

The sensor is placed at the point representing average field conditions — not the best or worst spot.

Control vs. Monitoring Distinction

The control sensor that triggers decisions and the monitoring sensor that validates the system are placed at different locations.

Quote Evaluation

The real tests of an automation system

Questions to ask when evaluating a quote:

Does irrigation continue if the internet goes down?

The field unit must be able to run the last programme locally.

What happens if there is no power?

Field units must be able to operate on solar and battery power.

Was the range measured or assumed?

Wireless range is the value measured in the field, not the figure stated in the catalogue.

Will the system notice if a valve stays open?

Without flow measurement and logic interlocks, a stuck-open valve can go undetected for days.

Can you access your data?

When the contract ends, does your data remain yours? Can you export it?

Who will operate the system?

If the person on the ground cannot use it, the system will quickly be switched to manual mode.
