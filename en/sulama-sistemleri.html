How is the system type selected?

"The irrigation method is not a trend — it is a result. Four inputs determine the decision: crop, soil, water, and topography."

System Types

Which system, under which conditions?

Every system type has a strength and a risk threshold. The right decision is project-specific.

Drip Irrigation

The method of delivering water directly to the root zone at low flow rates and frequent intervals. It is the default choice for row-planted crops. However, it requires clean water and regular maintenance: if filtration is inadequate or flushing is not carried out, the most expensive system becomes the fastest one to fail.

Micro Sprinkler

Wets a larger area than drip irrigation. Stands out for trees with spreading root systems, sandy soils, and orchards with frost protection needs.

Sprinkler Irrigation & Big Gun

For field crops, turf and green areas, pre-irrigation, and soil softening. Wind effect and evaporation losses are high; energy costs are also higher due to operating pressure.

Subsurface Drip Irrigation (SDI)

The system where the drip line is buried below the soil surface. Evaporation loss is nearly zero, farm operations do not snag the pipes, and system lifespan is long. On the other hand, if root intrusion and clogging risk are not managed, recovery is difficult — and a fault is invisible. Very powerful in the right project, an expensive mistake in the wrong one.

When evaluating your quote

Questions to ask about an irrigation project

"In an irrigation project, the decisions that determine cost and lifespan are well-defined. Ask the questions below — whoever you ask them to."

1.Is a pressure-compensating dripper necessary? +
Required on sloped land: elevation difference changes pressure along the line, and in a non-compensating dripper this creates a flow rate difference between the top and bottom of the field. A pressure-compensating dripper delivers a constant flow rate within a defined pressure range. On flat land it is an unnecessary cost. The decision is made by elevation measurement.
2.Is the zone size correct? +
Zones are limited by the instantaneous flow rate of the water source. If a zone is too large, sufficient pressure does not reach the end of the line; if too small, valve, control, and labour costs increase and the irrigation cycle grows longer.
3.Was filtration selected based on water analysis? +
Filter type and mesh opening are derived from the water analysis and the flow cross-section of the selected dripper. A filter quote given without analysis is a guess.
4.Has the pump operating point been verified? +
The pump operates at the point where flow rate and total manometric head intersect. If this point lies outside the efficiency zone on the pump curve, the system will either underperform or draw excess current. This is not noticed on day one; it shows up in the electricity bill and pump life.
5.Was it designed for today or for maturity? +
For perennial crops, the system will remain in place for many years. A system sized to the water requirements of the sapling stage will be inadequate once the trees reach full canopy and will require pipe replacement.
6.Is there room for expansion? +
If the main line and control unit are selected at full capacity for the current area, adding area later becomes nearly impossible. Reasonable spare capacity reduces the cost of the next investment significantly.
