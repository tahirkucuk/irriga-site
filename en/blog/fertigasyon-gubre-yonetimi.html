Fertiliser Savings with Fertigation: 17% Less Fertiliser, 30% More Yield

Have you ever wondered how much of the fertiliser you broadcast on your field actually reaches the plant? Most of the time, a large proportion remains suspended in the soil or is washed away into groundwater by rain. That is exactly what fertiliser savings with fertigation is designed to stop. Taking its name from "fertilization" and "irrigation", fertigation delivers plant nutrients directly to the root zone together with the irrigation water. Studies conducted in Turkey show that this method achieves a 20–30% increase in yield and savings of approximately 17% in fertiliser consumption. In this article I will break down both the technical details and the figures that will make a real difference to your bottom line.

What Is Fertigation and Why Is It Growing in Turkey?

In its simplest form, fertigation means combining water and fertiliser in a single system. Calibrated doses of fertiliser are mixed into the water flowing through drip irrigation pipes, and this mixture is delivered directly to the plant's roots via the emitters. The uncertainty of broadcast fertilisation — "how much of this will actually work?" — disappears entirely.

So why is it spreading so rapidly? Because fertiliser is a significant item in agricultural inputs. Today in Turkey, fertiliser accounts for approximately 15–20% of total agricultural inputs. Imagine cutting that share by 17%; the difference left in your pocket by harvest time is nothing to scoff at.

Turkey's soils are also well suited to this method. Fertigation offers targeted intervention for nutrient uptake problems such as iron chlorosis, which is commonly seen in the calcareous Mediterranean soils typical of much of Turkey. You can deliver exactly what the plant needs, at exactly the right moment, in exactly the right dose. Another important benefit is environmental: because you use less fertiliser, the leaching of nutrients into groundwater — and the associated pollution — is significantly reduced.

Fertiliser Savings with Fertigation: What Do the Numbers Say?

Let's stop talking and look at the numbers. Field studies conducted in Turkey have clearly demonstrated the tangible gains of fertigation. The most striking data concerns fertiliser use efficiency: the effect you achieve with 50 kg of fertiliser by broadcast application can be achieved with only 10–15 kg of fertiliser via fertigation. In other words, the same outcome is possible with 3–5 times less fertiliser.

💡 Expert Tip:

To maximise fertiliser savings, start with a soil and water analysis. Draw up a weekly fertilisation programme according to the phenological stage of the crop; follow the "little and often" principle by giving small doses with every irrigation. A single large dose creates a salinity risk and cancels out the savings advantage.

Drip Fertigation System: Flow Rate and Pressure Details

A good drip fertigation system is built on the right flow rate and pressure values. An incorrectly chosen emitter or insufficient pressure leads to one end of the field receiving too much water while the other end remains dry. Because fertiliser is carried with the water, this imbalance directly disrupts fertilisation uniformity.

Flow rate selection: The most common emitter flow rates in standard drip irrigation pipes are 2, 4 and 8 LPH (litres per hour). Manufacturers use colour coding; for example, a black emitter indicates 4 L/h and a blue emitter indicates 2 L/h. The appropriate one is chosen based on crop type and soil structure. With adjustable emitters, fine adjustment is possible in the range of 0 to 70 litres per hour; these emitters can be fitted to 16 mm and 20 mm drip pipes by punching a 3 mm hole.

Pressure setting: As a general rule, standard drip irrigation systems operate in the range of 1.0 to 2.0 bar (approximately 14.5–29 PSI). This range ensures both that water exits the emitters uniformly and that excessive load is not imposed on pipes, fittings and valves. Also note that lateral pipes must be made of PE material and able to withstand a minimum operating pressure of PN 4.

If your land is sloped, pressure-compensating (PC) emitters are critical for you. PC emitters maintain a constant outlet flow rate (e.g. 2 L/h) even when the inlet pressure varies between 1 bar and 4 bar. This way, every plant at the top of the slope or at the bottom of the hillside receives equal water and equal fertiliser.

Irrigation Fertilisation Methods and NPK Application

Irrigation fertilisation methods are generally built on three main technologies: the Venturi principle, a magnetic dosing pump, and a mechanical diaphragm dosing pump.

  • Venturi system: Requires a differential pressure to be created at the system inlet and outlet and a stable water pressure to be maintained. Simple and economical; however, the constant-pressure requirement is a constraint.
  • Dosing pump systems: No need to create differential pressure or maintain constant pressure. Calibrated pumps allow dosing at any desired quantity and rate. A typical dosing pump can deliver a flow rate of 1.5 to 60 litres per hour, adjust the dosing ratio up to 3,000:1, and have a suction capacity of more than 6 metres.

So how is NPK fertilisation carried out with drip irrigation? The first rule is to use fully soluble fertilisers; otherwise the emitters will clog. You adjust the NPK ratio according to the crop's growth stage: nitrogen-heavy formulas are preferred during vegetative growth, phosphorus- and potassium-heavy formulas during flowering and fruit set. After dissolving the fertiliser in the main tank, inject it into the system using a dosing pump or Venturi, then flush the system with clean water for a few minutes at the end of irrigation to prevent fertiliser residues from remaining inside the pipes.

💡 Expert Tip:

Be careful when mixing different fertilisers in the same tank. Never combine calcium-containing fertilisers with phosphate- and sulphate-containing ones in the same tank; they form precipitates that clog the emitters. Using two separate tanks is the safest solution.

Government Grants: Up to 70% Subsidy Available

If you are put off by the cost of switching to fertigation, the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry's Water-Saving Agricultural Irrigation Systems (TTS) Grant is exactly what you need. Depending on the nature of the investment, the grant rate ranges from 50% to 70%.

  • Scope: Drip, sprinkler and micro-sprinkler systems; as well as subsurface drip irrigation, solar-powered irrigation and smart irrigation/automation systems.
  • Upper limit: Projects with a goods purchase amount eligible for the grant of up to 10,000,000 TL are supported.
  • Application: Carried out entirely online at kirsalkalkinma.tarimorman.gov.tr.
  • Monitoring: A monitoring process continues for 5 years after the investment is completed, with active use of the systems being supervised.

The key documents you need to prepare for the application are: Soil Physical Analysis Report, Irrigation Water Analysis Report, Water Source Use Permit / Groundwater Use Certificate and Criminal Record Certificate. Preparing these documents early will make your application process considerably smoother.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is fertigation and how is it applied?
Fertigation is the delivery of plant nutrients directly to the root zone together with the irrigation water. In practice, fully soluble fertilisers are dissolved in a main tank, injected into the drip irrigation line via a Venturi or dosing pump, and delivered to the plant via the emitters. The line is flushed with clean water at the end of irrigation.
How much fertiliser saving does fertigation provide?
Studies in Turkey show approximately 17% savings in fertiliser consumption. In terms of use efficiency, the effect achieved with 50 kg of fertiliser by broadcast application can be achieved with 10–15 kg via fertigation — a difference in efficiency of 3–5 times.
How much does fertigation reduce fertiliser costs?
Since fertiliser accounts for 15–20% of total agricultural inputs, a saving of approximately 17% in fertiliser directly affects this line item. When you add the 20–30% increase in yield, the fertiliser cost per unit of production drops noticeably.
Can fertigation be carried out on sloped land?
Yes. Pressure-compensating (PC) emitters are used on sloped land. These emitters maintain a constant outlet flow rate even when the inlet pressure varies between 1 and 4 bar, ensuring equal water and fertiliser distribution at every point on the field.
