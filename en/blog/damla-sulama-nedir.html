What Is Drip Irrigation and How Does It Work?

📅 15 August 2026 ⏱️ 5 min read ✍️ Irriga Engineering
📌 What's in This Guide?

  2. 2. How Does It Work?

  4. 4. Advantages

Drip irrigation is a modern irrigation method that delivers water directly to the plant root zone at low flow rates and continuously through pipelines and emitters. Compared to conventional furrow or surface irrigation, it consumes 40–60% less water while significantly improving yield and product quality. The widespread adoption of drip irrigation in Turkey — particularly in high-value horticultural crops and protected cultivation — has brought about a revolutionary leap in productivity.

1. What Is Drip Irrigation?

Drip irrigation is a micro-irrigation system that delivers reduced-pressure water flow drop by drop to the exact root zone of each plant through very small-orifice emitters, drippers or in-line emitters. Applying water only to the area where the plant needs it minimises both weed growth and surface evaporation.

💡 Key Difference:

In surface irrigation, only 45–55% of the water reaches the plant; the rest is lost to evaporation, deep percolation or surface runoff. In drip irrigation, this ratio rises to 90–95%.

2. How Does It Work?

The operating logic of the system is based on five fundamental stages:

  1. Pump & Pressure Regulation: Water drawn from the source (well, canal, mains) is fed into the system under pressure by the pump station. Pressure regulators keep the system balanced between 0.5–2 bar.
  2. Filtration Unit: Hydrocyclone and disc/sand filters remove coarse and fine particles to prevent clogging of the emitters.
  3. Fertigation Tank (if present): A fertiliser injector (Venturi or dosing pump) mixes soluble fertilisers into the irrigation water and carries them to the root zone.
  4. Mainline & Lateral Pipes: PVC mainlines and PE lateral pipes distribute water across the field.
  5. Emitters: Drippers placed next to each plant release water at an adjustable flow rate of 2–8 litres per hour.

3. Key Components

Pump Station

The pump, which is the heart of the system, is selected according to the size and topography of the land. Electric submersible pumps are commonly preferred in well applications, while diesel centrifugal pumps are widely used on remote fields.

Filtration System

Filtration appropriate to the water quality is essential to prevent emitter orifices from clogging. A hydrocyclone + disc filter combination is standard for sandy water sources. Sand-gravel filters provide extra protection for lake and canal water with high organic matter loads.

Emitter Types

  • On-line Emitter: Mounted on the lateral pipe via a connection fitting; ideal for orchards.
  • In-Line Emitter: Emitters integrated during pipe manufacturing; the most common choice for field crops and greenhouse applications.
  • Pressure-Compensating Emitter: Maintains constant flow at inlet pressures of 0.5–4 bar; indispensable for uniform irrigation on sloped land.
  • Micro Sprinkler & Jet: Semi-drip solutions that wet a wider root zone; preferred in nurseries and young orchards.

4. Advantages

  • Water Savings: 40–60% less water consumption compared to surface irrigation.
  • Yield Increase: Uniform moisture distribution reduces root stress; typically 20–35% yield increase is observed.
  • Fertiliser Efficiency: Fertigation delivers fertiliser directly to the root zone, minimising waste.
  • Weed Reduction: The soil between plants remains dry; weed seeds do not germinate.
  • Disease Control: Keeping leaves and fruit dry prevents the spread of fungal diseases in particular.
  • Land Use: Tractors and machinery can continue working in the field during irrigation.
📊 Real Case:

A pressure-compensating drip irrigation system installed by Irriga on a 45-decare olive grove in the Izmir region reduced the producer's water bill by 52% in the first season and delivered a 28% increase in early harvest and yield.

5. Which Land Types Is It Suitable For?

Drip irrigation can be applied on almost every type of land, but it is unrivalled especially under the following conditions:

  • Sloped and uneven land: Pressure-compensating emitters ensure uniform irrigation across the entire plot.
  • Areas with limited water resources: The only viable option on land with low irrigation quotas.
  • High-value crops: Tomatoes, strawberries, table grapes, olives, citrus fruits, orchards.
  • Protected cultivation: Fully controlled growing in greenhouses with fertigation integration.
  • Sandy and permeable soils: On soils where water escapes rapidly downward, frequent-interval drip irrigation optimises water retention.

The disadvantages of drip irrigation include initial investment cost and the need for regular maintenance. However, with proper engineering design and quality equipment selection, a system life of 10–15 years converts the investment into a clear net gain.
