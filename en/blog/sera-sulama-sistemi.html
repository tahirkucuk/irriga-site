Key Considerations for Greenhouse Irrigation System Installation

📅 15 June 2026 ⏱️ 5 min read ✍️ Irriga Engineering

Greenhouse cultivation offers a far more controlled environment compared to open-field production — but this control must be supported by the right irrigation system. An incorrectly sized or poorly installed greenhouse irrigation system leads to humidity imbalances, root diseases and yield decline. In this guide we cover the critical points of irrigation engineering specific to the greenhouse environment.

1. Why Is Greenhouse Irrigation Different?

Unlike open-field irrigation, in a greenhouse:

  • There is no natural rainfall effect; every drop of water is supplied by you.
  • The enclosed environment creates ideal conditions for fungal diseases due to moisture build-up.
  • Plant density is high; uniform water delivery to every plant is critical.
  • Fertilisation is carried out simultaneously with irrigation (fertigation).
  • The transpiration rate — which depends on temperature and light intensity — varies by the hour.
💡 Expert Tip:

In a greenhouse, overwatering is more dangerous than underwatering. Waterlogging causes root suffocation and creates ideal conditions for lethal root rot diseases such as Pythium.

2. Humidity Control and Microclimate

The effect of the irrigation system on humidity management in a greenhouse has two dimensions: soil moisture and air humidity.

Soil / Substrate Moisture

Different substrates — mineral soil, coir (cocopeat), perlite and rock wool — have different water-holding capacities. Drip irrigation flow rate and frequency must be adjusted according to the substrate. With cocopeat, 6–8 short irrigation cycles per day are appropriate, while 2–3 longer cycles are sufficient for mineral soil.

Air Humidity

Using micro-sprinklers or overhead irrigation increases greenhouse air humidity to dangerous levels. For this reason, subsurface or in-row drip irrigation is preferred in greenhouses. When leaf and fruit surfaces remain dry, the risk of Botrytis (grey mould) and downy mildew drops significantly.

3. Fertigation Integration

The greatest advantage of greenhouse irrigation is that fertiliser can be added to the irrigation water and delivered directly to the root zone. This system is known as fertigation and, when used efficiently, increases fertiliser efficacy by 40–60%.

Dosing Pump Selection

Precision dosing pumps such as the ITC DOSmart series measure EC (electrical conductivity) and pH values in real time and automatically adjust the fertiliser ratio. Manual mixing tanks offer a cost advantage for small greenhouses, but do not guarantee uniform concentration.

EC and pH Management

In a greenhouse, the EC value of the irrigation water should be kept between 1.5–4.0 mS/cm depending on plant type and growth stage. pH should remain in the 5.5–6.5 range; outside this range, nutrient elements become locked and the plant cannot absorb fertiliser through the roots.

📋 Application Note:

Fertigation systems installed in Irriga projects using an ITC dosing pump + EC/pH meter combination achieve an average of 35% fertiliser savings and a 22% yield increase compared to conventional fertilised irrigation practices.

4. Automation and Scheduling

In greenhouse irrigation, automation is a critical investment both for water efficiency and labour cost reduction.

Timer Control

The plant's water demand changes dramatically depending on light intensity throughout the day. Irrigation durations for early morning, midday and evening must be programmed according to the plant's daily cycle.

Sensor-Based Control

Integration of soil moisture sensors or tensiometers enables irrigation based on actual need. Platforms such as WiseConn independently manage multiple zones within a greenhouse and transmit all data to the cloud.

Weather Station Integration

Indoor light meters (pyranometers) estimate transpiration and automatically increase or reduce the irrigation volume. This system is called ET-based (evapotranspiration) irrigation and represents the most advanced greenhouse control approach.

5. Common Installation Mistakes

  • Insufficient Filtration: Fertiliser added to greenhouse water creates organic sediment. Disc filters must be at least 120 mesh thickness.
  • Uneven Pressure Distribution: Pressure drop in long lateral lines causes flow differences exceeding 30% between the first and last plants. Pressure-compensating emitters are mandatory.
  • Incorrect Emitter Placement: Placing the emitter at the edge of the root development zone rather than directly on top of the pot or substrate block encourages active root growth.
  • Forgetting Drain Valves: Water remaining in lateral pipes during winter shutdown can freeze in Turkey's colder regions and crack the pipes.
  • Continuous Long Irrigation Cycles: Small amounts of water delivered very frequently keeps the root zone at the surface. The correct practice supports deeper root development.
